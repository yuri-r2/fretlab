    How practice works
    • New material in one block, review mixed together. Today's plan gives one new exercise step a solid block of repetition, then mixes short reviews of different skills. In studies with clarinetists, mixing exercises held up better than repeating one at a time when players were tested later, even though most players preferred blocked practice (Stambaugh, 2011; Carter and Grahn, 2016). Results depend on the task: Mathias and Goldman, 2025 found better retention with blocked practice of difficult violin excerpts. Use focused repetition for an unfamiliar task and manageable contrasts as it becomes comfortable.
    • Clean runs, not just more runs. In a study of advanced pianists, total practice time and repetitions didn't predict next-day performance. The best players fixed errors on the spot, changed tempo on purpose and repeated a passage until it stayed right (Duke, Simmons and Cash, 2009). The two-clean-run rule and slowdown of about 10% are practical defaults inspired by this approach, rather than experimentally established thresholds. In technical steps, the tempo rises after two clean runs, and "Practice" lets you loop just the bar that breaks, then put it back into the whole step.
    • Listen before you play. Musicians who heard a model before practicing improved more during practice and overnight (Cash and colleagues). New steps suggest one "Listen first" pass.
    • Spaced, cold reviews. How you play during practice is a poor guide to what you'll keep (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015). Finished steps and passed units come back after 1, 3, 7, 16 and 35 days, with a first-play check before listening or rehearsal. Prepared attempts return tomorrow. These day intervals are practical defaults, rather than a validated guitar-specific schedule. Spacing reviews out helps long-term memory, and the best gap grows the longer you want to remember something (Cepeda et al., 2008). Recalling from memory also beats re-reading (Karpicke and Roediger).
    • Focus on the sound. Musicians played more expressively, and no less accurately, when they focused on the sound they were making instead of their finger movements (Mornell and Wulf, 2018). Each exercise gives one listening cue.
    • Write your own, inside limits. Popular musicians learn by ear and by making their own music alongside practice (Lucy Green). Every voicing ends with a "Your riff" step, and a small constraint helps you avoid falling back on old habits.
    • Plan, then reflect. Musicians who were taught to set a goal and check their own progress improved more in the same practice time (Miksza). After a routine, write one line about what to fix next time; it shows up at the start of your next session.
    • Ears in context. Scale-degree training plays a cadence first, then the note, then resolves it home. It's the approach popularized by the Functional Ear Trainer app.

      What counts as clean?

      Clean: no stops, every note lands with the click (one tiny slip is fine). Almost: kept going, a few notes off. Messy: stopped or lost the beat. Two clean runs in a row move the tempo up.
